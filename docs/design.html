Design note · Interledger × Evernode

Nomad Connector

An Interledger connector that runs as a HotPocket contract on Evernode: nobody operates it, peers pay for it as they use it, and it pays its own hosts in EVR from a Xahau account only the cluster can sign for.

prototype date 2 September 2026 code nomad-connector.zip (contract · plugin · simulator · 18 tests · real 3-node cluster run) origin forum.interledger.org/t/126, March 2019

§1Summary

The 2019 idea is buildable in 2026, and the half of it that sounded hardest — a contract with no operator that keeps itself hosted — is the half Evernode has already solved. What remains hard is that a connector is custodial, and that the law has moved from "is it decentralised?" to "who has control?".

This note describes a working prototype: a deterministic ILP connector core that runs unchanged on a simulated three-node HotPocket cluster and on a real one (three hpdevkit nodes in Docker, where the unmodified ilp-protocol-stream library paid 1 XAH from a peer on node 1 to a peer on node 2 in 5.8 s), an ilp-plugin-hotpocket that makes that possible, a settlement design built on Xahau payment channels and the cluster's multisig account, and a treasury loop that keeps the hosts paid in EVR. The bridge to Xahau is written against everpocket's real API and exercised through a fake; it has not yet been run against Xahau testnet.

Recommendation: treat this as an alternative ILP network for crypto-native peers, not as a node on the Interledger Foundation's Rafiki network; engineer every remaining design decision around the "non-controlling" test now in front of the U.S. Senate; and take the next step on Xahau testnet with three real hosts before adding liquidity providers or a second asset.

§2The vision, and what changed since 2019

It seems to me that connectors are necessarily money service businesses, but AML requirements would make running a connector almost impossible. The obvious option (to me at least) is to have connectors run as decentralised contracts on codius or similar where no one party runs the contract, and all parties who use it pay for it as they use it, and it in turn pays for its own resource usage.codetsunami, “What is the incentive for running a connector?”, Interledger forum, 7 March 2019

Three things are different now. Codius is gone and Evernode has taken its place: a HotPocket cluster can own a Xahau account outright, with one signer key per node kept outside consensus state and an 80 %-weight quorum on the account's SignerList, and everpocket's Nomad cluster model lets "the DApp itself completely take control of managing the cluster" — buying, extending and replacing its own leases from that account. That is the second half of the quotation, almost verbatim.

Interledger went the other way. The network in 2026 is Rafiki and Open Payments: bilateral peering between regulated account-servicing entities, negotiated settlement, shared auth tokens. A compliance team at such an entity has no counterparty to diligence when the other side is a contract. So a decentralised connector serves a different market than the one the forum post imagined: wallets and services on XRPL and Xahau that already settle in crypto and do not need a legal person across the table.

And the law moved. "No one party runs it" is no longer the test anyone applies; control is. §8 takes that up.

§3Architecture

The connector is a hub. HotPocket contracts cannot deterministically open outbound connections, so every peer connects in over the HotPocket user channel, authenticated by its own ed25519 key; that key is the peer's identity and the tail of its ILP address. The cluster's nodes run the same deterministic core each consensus round, vote over the node-party-line (NPL) on anything they had to observe from outside, and co-sign the transactions the core decided on.

§4A packet through the cluster

HotPocket batches inputs into rounds, so a packet crossing the connector costs two rounds at minimum: the Prepare arrives in one round and is forwarded in that round's outputs; the Fulfill arrives in a later round and the reply goes back in that round's outputs. Expiry is judged against the consensus timestamp, never a node's clock, and the outgoing packet expires one window earlier than the incoming one so the connector is never left holding a fulfilled outgoing packet with an expired incoming one.

Latency budget

Measured on the real local cluster (1 s rounds, 4 s minimum window): the STREAM handshake — ILDCP plus the library's five exchange-rate probes, each a two-round trip — takes 9.0 s, and the 1 XAH payment itself 5.8 s. With the Evernode example config (3 s rounds, 8 s window) expect roughly three times that; a STREAM packet with the library's default 30 s expiry still clears the connector's expiresAt > now + 2w check. The simulator runs 25–40 ms rounds, which is why its demo pays 2.5 XAH end to end in about 250 ms. On real hosts, throughput comes from the hundreds of packets a round can carry, not from per-packet speed.

Rounds that touch the ledger take longer, because each node queries Xahau and the cluster votes. The bridge only does that every factsEvery rounds or when a settlement is pending, so packet-only rounds stay short.

§5Money model

The design refuses to lend. A peer funds itself by opening a Xahau payment channel to the connector's multisig account and streaming signed claims; the contract verifies each claim with pure crypto (every node reaches the same verdict without asking the ledger), credits it immediately, and redeems channels on-ledger in batches — or the moment a channel's owner starts closing it, because an unredeemed claim on a closed channel is a loss. Fulfilled packets move value between peer balances minus the fee. When a peer is owed more than a threshold, or asks, the cluster multisigns a Payment to the address the peer registered — but only from funds already on the ledger; unredeemed claims never back a payout.

Why channels in, plain payments out

Inbound channels are ideal: the peer signs claims with its own key and the contract only ever holds signatures, which the cluster can redeem with a normal multisigned PaymentChannelClaim. Outbound channels would be better than plain payments — instant, no per-payout fee — but a channel's claims must be signed by a single key, and the cluster deliberately has no single key. Threshold signatures (a FROST-style ed25519 scheme across the nodes) would close that gap; until then, outbound settlement is a batched multisigned Payment.

Claims are bound to the first peer that presents them, a channel's settle delay must be at least an hour before its claims are accepted, and funds the owner pushes on-ledger directly are credited once by fast-forwarding the claim watermark, never double-counted. All three rules have tests.

§6Determinism and the fact pipeline

HotPocket reaches consensus on users, inputs, outputs and the state hash. The contract therefore has to be a pure function of what the cluster agreed on, and anything a node learns on its own — the account balance, which channels exist, whether a transaction validated — must go through a vote before it can touch state. The core never reads a clock, never opens a socket and never uses randomness; the bridge does all of that, and returns facts only after an NPL election picks the majority view (ties broken by hash, so every node picks the same one).

The same discipline applies on the way out. The core emits intents — transactions it wants signed — and everpocket's multiSignAndSubmitTransaction decides the sequence number by vote, collects the signer nodes' signatures over NPL, has one node submit, and votes the result back so every node applies the same outcome. A payout is debited when it is planned, refunded if submission fails, and confirmed only when a later round's facts show it validated.

The simulator enforces the property the platform would: after every round it hashes each node's state directory and compares outputs; the first disagreement stops the cluster. One test tampers with a node's state file on purpose and checks that the fork is caught; the others, including the STREAM payment and the fake-everpocket bridge run, finish with no fork across all rounds. On the real cluster the three HotPocket nodes reported identical ledger and state hashes every round (for example lcl:158-8ffa2042 state:bd17d692 on nodes 1 and 3), with the contract taking about 100 ms of each 1 s round.

One caveat belongs here. everpocket keeps its own bookkeeping (transactions.json, cluster data) in consensus state, and writes it from per-node ledger queries without a vote. The connector's state cannot diverge through that path, but a node whose everpocket files diverge will still fall out of sync. It is the kind of thing a testnet run will surface.

§7The self-funding loop

Evernode hosts are paid per moment (about an hour) in EVR, and a lease that is not extended ends with the instance. everpocket's NomadContext already implements the loop the forum post asked for: each round it prunes nodes that went quiet, grows the cluster back to its target size by acquiring leases from preferred hosts, and extends leases that are within a randomised margin of expiry — all paid from the multisig account, with signer keys added or replaced on the SignerList as nodes come and go.

The connector's part is to keep that account in EVR. Fees accrue in XAH; when the EVR balance drops under the reserve and there is free equity (on-ledger balance minus the reserve minus everything owed to peers), the treasury places an immediate-or-cancel offer on the Xahau DEX to buy EVR, and backs off for twenty rounds if the book cannot fill it at the limit price. In the simulated run the cluster pays three hosts 24 EVR for lease extensions and then restores its reserve from fees. On mainnet that DEX depth is an assumption to verify.

Nothing in the loop needs a person. That is the point, and it is also the part that raises the question in the next section.

§8Control, not decentralisation

"No one party runs the contract" was a fair summary of the 2019 hope. Since then Roman Storm was convicted (August 2025) of conspiracy to operate an unlicensed money-transmitting business over non-custodial software, with a retrial of the deadlocked counts scheduled for October 2026; the government's theory turned on what the developers still controlled and profited from. Going the other way, the Blockchain Regulatory Certainty Act (Lummis/Wyden, January 2026; also §604 of the CLARITY Act, which passed the House in July 2025, cleared Senate Banking in May 2026 and faces a Senate vote this month) would exempt a "non-controlling developer or provider": someone without "the legal right or unilateral ability to control, initiate, or effectuate transactions involving users' digital assets without third-party approval."

That definition is a design specification. The table below maps it onto the prototype.

Mapping the design onto a statutory definition is engineering hygiene, not a legal opinion, and the definition is not yet law. Two further points stand regardless: the hosts are closer to the money than a cloud provider is (they hold signing keys), and the peers, not the contract, are where any obligation to know a counterparty lands. Anyone taking this beyond a testnet should get advice from a lawyer who has read the Storm rulings and the BRCA text.

§10What the prototype shows

Then the same contract on a real cluster.

§11Open questions and next steps

Before anyone relies on it

  • Run it on Evernode. The local cluster run settled the HotPocket half (consensus, user channel, timing). Next is a three-host cluster on Xahau testnet through evdevkit cluster-create --signer-count 3, the tenant key retired, one peer funding through a real channel — where everpocket's rough edges and the DEX assumptions get measured instead of assumed.
  • Liquidity providers. Fees alone make the float grow slowly. An LP deposit with shares and withdrawal rights — and nothing else — keeps the control posture intact; anything resembling governance rights over the pool does not.
  • Outbound channels. Threshold ed25519 signing across the nodes would let the cluster sign channel claims to peers, making payouts instant and fee-free.
  • Upgrades. "No upgrade path" is the safest posture and the least practical one. Options are hash-pinned versions that peers opt into by reconnecting, or a migration message that moves balances to a successor contract the peers name — never an admin key.

Later

  • Multi-asset routing (XRP over XRPL, IOUs), which needs per-asset ledgers and an agreed rate source.
  • Multi-hop: letting a peer that is itself a connector announce prefixes, with the hijack problem solved by bonded announcements.
  • Host selection and diversity policy for the Nomad loop, and what a bond or insurance layer for the custodial pool would look like.
  • Receipts: STREAM receipts signed by the receiver would let peers audit the connector's fees without trusting it.
